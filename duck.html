Overview

I am working on a variant of Cops and Robbers, a pursuit-evasion game on graphs, inspired by the chess variant Duck Chess.

In Duck Chess, each player finishes their turn by places the duck onto an empty square. Wherever it lands, the duck blocks pieces from moving onto its square and, for pieces that move along a line, from traveling through it.

Exactly how that functions depends on the piece: a rook can lose a whole stretch of possible moves, while a knight only cares if the duck has stolen its landing spot. See figures below for an example of the duck blocking each piece.

The Game of Ducks and Robbers

We use this idea to create a new game, Ducks and Robbers, played on what we call a duck graph.

Duck graphs are a little like hypergraphs. A hypergraph has hyperedges, which are sets of vertices. A duck graph instead has lanes: ordered sequences of vertices. That order tells us not only which vertices are connected, but which ones lie in between, and therefore exactly where a duck can get in the way.

Canonical Duck Graphs

Chess graphs come with their lanes built in. For graphs that do not arrive with a chess piece attached, we use the canonical duck graph: every edge becomes a two-vertex lane, with one-vertex lanes for any isolated vertices. The duck therefore blocks only the vertex it is sitting on. This gives us a standard way to bring every graph family into the game.

Our results for canonical duck graphs include:

  • Cycles: for n at least 5, the duck cop number of Cn is 2.
  • Cocktail party graphs: the duck cop number is 1, even though the classical cop number is 2.
  • Trees: the duck cop number is equal to the number of support vertices in the tree.
  • Hypercubes: the n-dimensional hypercube Qn has duck cop number ⌊n/2⌋.
  • Disconnected graphs: the duck cop number is the sum of the duck cop numbers of the connected components.

Characterization of Duck Graphs Where k Cops Can Win

We characterize the duck graphs where k cops can win, in a way similar to the characterization of ordinary k-cop-win graphs in this paper.

A concise statement is the following. Let y = (y1, …, yk) and [y] = {y1, …, yk}. Then

x ≼0k y ⇔ x ∈ [y].

x ≼αk y ⇔ ∀ d1 ∈ V(D) ∖ ({x} ∪ [y]), ∃ y′ ∈ ∏i=1k Nd1[yi] :

x ∈ [y′] ∨ ∃ d2 ∈ V(D) ∖ ({x} ∪ [y′]), ∀ x′ ∈ Nd2[x], ∃ β < α : x′ ≼βk y′  (α > 0).

≼k := ≼ρk,  ρ = min{α : ≼αk = ≼α+1k}.

D is k-duck-cop-win ⇔ ∃ y ∈ V(D)k ∀ x ∈ V(D) : x ≼k y.

Have fun deciphering that.

Chess Graph Results

And now, back to the pieces that started all of this:

  • Kings: for m, n > 1, the m × n king duck graph has duck cop number 1.
  • Rooks: for m, n ≥ 4, the m × n rook duck graph has duck cop number 2.
  • Bishops: for m, n ≥ 4, the duck cop number is 6 when m and n are both odd, and 4 otherwise.
  • Knights: the duck cop number is greater than 2. We do not have an upper bound yet.
  • Queens: the duck cop number is either 6 or 7.

Cop Number and Duck Cop Number

Lastly, we show by construction that, for any positive integers p and k, there is a graph G with duck cop number p and classical cop number k.
